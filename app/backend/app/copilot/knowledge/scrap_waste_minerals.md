# ScrapTrust domain knowledge base

Curated reference notes used by the Copilot (search_knowledge_base tool and offline answers).
Each "## " heading is one retrievable entry. Keep entries factual and general; no prices.

## Ferrous vs non-ferrous metals and the magnet test
Ferrous metals contain iron (carbon steel, cast iron, most stainless 400-series) and are attracted to a magnet. Non-ferrous metals (aluminium, copper, brass, bronze, lead, zinc, nickel alloys, titanium) are not magnetic and are usually worth more per kilogram. A magnet is the first sorting test in any yard. Caution: austenitic stainless steel (300-series such as 304 and 316) is non-magnetic or only weakly magnetic, especially after cold working, so a non-magnetic grey metal is not automatically aluminium. Check weight (aluminium is about one third the density of steel) and colour, and confirm grades with XRF.

## Aluminium scrap types and sorting
Keep aluminium separated by type, because buyers pay differently for each: extrusions (window frames, typically 6063 alloy), clean sheet, cast aluminium (engine parts, wheels; higher silicon), used beverage cans (UBC), and aluminium radiators or wire. Wrought and cast alloys should not be mixed. Value falls with contamination: paint, plastic, rubber, steel screws and inserts, oil and dirt. Remove steel attachments (check with a magnet) and keep the material dry. Alloy grade (for example 6061 versus 6063) cannot be seen in a photo; it needs XRF or OES.

## Copper scrap grades
Copper is graded mainly by purity and condition. Clean, bright, uncoated unalloyed copper wire or tube is the top grade. Copper with solder, paint, light oxidation or fittings attached is a lower grade. Insulated copper wire is valued on its expected copper recovery percentage, which depends on wire gauge and insulation thickness. Copper must be free of brass fittings, steel and excessive insulation to reach the better grades. Copper is reddish-orange, non-magnetic and heavy; green patina indicates oxidation.

## Brass and bronze
Brass is a copper–zinc alloy (yellow colour: plumbing fittings, valves, keys, shell casings). Bronze is mainly copper–tin, often with other elements (reddish-brown: bearings, bushings, marine fittings, statues). Both are non-magnetic. Red brass or gunmetal contains more copper and is valued above yellow brass. Separate brass from copper and remove steel parts. Exact composition requires XRF; colour alone is unreliable, especially on tarnished surfaces.

## Stainless steel grades
Common stainless grades are 304 (18% chromium, 8% nickel) and 316 (adds about 2% molybdenum for corrosion resistance), both austenitic and weakly magnetic or non-magnetic, and 400-series ferritic or martensitic grades that are magnetic and contain little or no nickel. Nickel and molybdenum content drive value, so 316 is worth more than 304, and both more than 400-series. These grades look identical; only XRF or OES separates them reliably.

## Lead scrap
Lead is dull grey, very heavy (density about 11.3 g/cm³), soft enough to scratch with a fingernail or knife, and non-magnetic. Sources include wheel weights, cable sheathing, roofing flashing, radiation shielding and lead-acid batteries. Lead is toxic: wear gloves, avoid dust and fumes, wash hands, and never melt it without proper ventilation and permits.

## Lithium-ion battery safety
Lithium-ion batteries (phones, laptops, power tools, e-bikes, EVs) can catch fire through thermal runaway if crushed, punctured, short-circuited, overheated or damaged. Never put them in a shredder, baler or compactor. Tape or cap the terminals, store them separately in a cool, dry place in non-combustible containers, and isolate swollen, leaking or damaged cells (for example in sand or vermiculite). Keep a fire plan: lithium battery fires can re-ignite. Transport is regulated as dangerous goods (UN3480/UN3481). Hidden batteries inside e-waste and toys are a common cause of yard and truck fires, so remove them before processing.

## Lead-acid batteries
Lead-acid batteries (vehicles, UPS, forklifts) contain lead plates and sulfuric acid. Keep them upright, do not drain or break them open, store them on acid-resistant surfaces away from drains, and send them to an authorised recycler. They are one of the most recycled products in the world: the lead, the polypropylene case and the acid (neutralised or converted) are all recovered.

## Other battery chemistries
Nickel–metal hydride (NiMH) batteries contain nickel and rare-earth alloys. Nickel–cadmium (NiCd) batteries contain toxic cadmium and must be collected separately. Alkaline and zinc–carbon primary batteries are lower hazard but should still be recycled for zinc, manganese and steel. Button cells may contain silver, lithium or, in older types, mercury. Sort batteries by chemistry before sending them to recyclers, since each process route is different.

## Battery recycling processes and black mass
Lithium-ion recycling typically starts with discharging and mechanical pre-treatment (dismantling, shredding under inert or controlled conditions, separating plastics, copper and aluminium foils) to produce "black mass", a powder containing lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese and graphite. Metals are then recovered by pyrometallurgy (high-temperature smelting; lithium often ends up in slag) or hydrometallurgy (acid leaching followed by solvent extraction and precipitation, which can recover lithium, nickel, cobalt and manganese salts at battery grade). Lead-acid batteries are recycled by breaking, separating lead paste, grids and plastics, and smelting or electrochemically refining the lead. Black-mass composition must be determined by lab assay (ICP-OES/ICP-MS), not visually.

## E-waste composition and hazards
Electronic waste (computers, phones, printed circuit boards, cables, appliances) contains valuable metals: copper, aluminium, steel, and precious metals such as gold, silver and palladium on circuit boards and connectors. It also contains hazards: lead solder, brominated flame retardants in plastics, mercury switches and backlights in older devices, leaded glass in CRT monitors, capacitors (very old ones may contain PCBs) and built-in batteries. Remove batteries first, keep CRTs intact, and send circuit boards to specialised refiners. Board value depends on board type (high-grade boards from servers and phones versus low-grade boards from TVs and power supplies).

## Fluorescent lamps, CFLs and light bulbs
Fluorescent tubes and compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) contain small amounts of mercury and must not be broken or put in general waste or glass recycling. Store them intact in boxes. If one breaks: ventilate the room, do not vacuum, collect fragments with stiff card and sticky tape, seal them in a container and send them to a lamp recycler. LED lamps contain no mercury but do contain electronics and should go to e-waste recycling. Incandescent and halogen bulbs are not accepted in container-glass recycling because their glass and metal parts contaminate it.

## Glass recycling
Container glass is sorted by colour: clear (flint), green and amber (brown). Mixing colours lowers value. Contaminants that ruin glass melts include ceramics, stones and porcelain (CSP), heat-resistant glass (Pyrex, cookware), window and windscreen glass (different chemistry, and windscreens are laminated), mirrors, light bulbs and CRT glass. Clean crushed glass is called cullet; using cullet reduces furnace energy and raw-material use.

## Plastics and resin identification codes
Resin codes: 1 PET (bottles), 2 HDPE (milk jugs, detergent bottles), 3 PVC (pipes, some packaging), 4 LDPE (films, bags), 5 PP (caps, tubs, automotive parts), 6 PS (foam, cups), 7 Other (polycarbonate, multilayer, bioplastics). PET and HDPE have the most established recycling markets. PVC contaminates PET recycling even in small amounts. Black plastics are hard to sort with near-infrared (NIR) sorters because carbon black absorbs the light. Keep plastics clean and dry, and separate films from rigid plastics.

## Paper and cardboard
Recovered paper grades include old corrugated containers (OCC, cardboard boxes), mixed paper, newspapers and office paper. Value drops with contamination: food and grease (pizza boxes), wax or plastic coatings, wet-strength papers, tape and staples in large amounts, and moisture. Keep bales dry, because wet paper loses value and can be rejected. Remove plastic film and polystyrene packing.

## Organic waste
Organic waste (food, garden waste, wood) can be composted or treated by anaerobic digestion to produce biogas and digestate. The main problem is contamination with plastics, glass and metals, which reduces compost quality. Treated or painted wood should not go into compost. Keep organic waste separate at source and avoid long storage, which causes odours and leachate.

## End-of-life vehicles and automobile waste
Before shredding, vehicles must be depolluted: drain fuel, engine oil, gearbox oil, coolant and brake fluid; recover air-conditioning refrigerant with certified equipment; remove the battery, airbags and seat-belt pretensioners (pyrotechnic devices), tyres, and parts containing mercury. Remove valuable parts such as catalytic converters, aluminium wheels, radiators, wiring harnesses (copper) and engines. After shredding, ferrous metal is separated magnetically, non-ferrous by eddy current, and the remainder (automotive shredder residue, ASR) is mostly plastics, foam, rubber and glass.

## Catalytic converters
Catalytic converters contain platinum-group metals (platinum, palladium, rhodium) coated on a ceramic or metallic honeycomb. Their value depends on the PGM loading, which varies by vehicle make, model and year, and is determined by sampling the decanned ceramic and assaying it (XRF for an estimate, lab fire assay or ICP for settlement). Aftermarket converters contain far less PGM than original (OEM) units. Keep converters intact until sale and document their origin, since converter theft is a common compliance concern.

## Contamination, moisture and yield deductions
Buyers deduct for anything that is not the paid material: moisture, oil, dirt, paint, plastic and rubber, attached steel on non-ferrous metals, and mixed grades. Clean, sorted, dry material sells at higher grades. Typical yard practice: separate by metal type, remove attachments, drain liquids, keep material out of the rain, and photograph loads with a scale reference for documentation.

## Practical identification tests for metals
Use several tests together: (1) magnet test; (2) colour and surface (copper red, brass yellow, aluminium light grey and light in weight, lead dull grey and very heavy, zinc bluish-grey); (3) density, by weighing a known volume; (4) spark test on a grinding wheel, which separates carbon steel, stainless and cast iron by spark pattern; (5) file or scratch test for hardness; (6) handheld XRF for alloy composition; (7) LIBS for light elements. Photos alone cannot determine alloy grade or chemistry.

## XRF, LIBS and lab analysis
Handheld X-ray fluorescence (XRF) identifies most alloy grades quickly and non-destructively, but it cannot detect very light elements such as lithium, beryllium and boron, and it measures carbon poorly, so it cannot grade carbon steels by carbon content. It needs a clean, flat, uncoated surface, because paint and plating distort readings. Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) can measure light elements, including lithium and in some instruments carbon, which makes it useful for aluminium–lithium alloys and some steels. Optical emission spectroscopy (OES) and lab methods (ICP-OES, ICP-MS, fire assay) give the most accurate results and are used for settlement of high-value material.

## Radioactive material in scrap
Scrap can occasionally contain radioactive sources (lost industrial or medical sources, contaminated pipes, luminous dials). Yards and mills use radiation portal monitors at the gate. If radiation is detected or a suspicious sealed capsule or labelled container is found: stop handling it, keep people away, do not cut or move it, and contact the radiation safety authority. Never melt or shred suspect material.

## Asbestos and other hazardous materials
Asbestos may be present in older brake pads and clutches, pipe and boiler insulation, cement sheets, floor tiles and some appliances. Do not break, cut or sand suspect material; it must be removed by licensed contractors. Other hazards to flag: oils and fuels in engines and tanks, pressurised cylinders and gas bottles (must be confirmed empty and valves removed), aerosols, sealed drums of unknown content, PCB-containing transformers and capacitors, and mercury devices.

## Refrigerators, freezers and air conditioners
Cooling appliances contain refrigerants (CFCs and HCFCs in older units, HFCs or hydrocarbons such as isobutane in newer ones) and insulating foam that may also contain blowing agents. Refrigerants must be recovered by certified technicians before scrapping; venting them is illegal in most jurisdictions. Compressors contain oil, and hydrocarbon refrigerants are flammable.

## Sorting technologies in recycling plants
Common separation methods: magnetic separators (ferrous); eddy-current separators (non-ferrous metals such as aluminium and copper ejected from a stream); near-infrared (NIR) sensor sorting (plastic types, paper); X-ray transmission (XRT) and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) sorting (metals and alloys by atomic density or composition); colour and optical sorters (glass and copper wire); density separation such as sink–float and air classification; and screening and trommels by size. Computer-vision AI sorting uses cameras and trained models to pick materials from conveyors.

## Photography guidelines for scrap evidence
For reliable photo assessment: use good, even daylight or diffuse lighting without strong shadows; capture the whole item or pile plus close-ups of surfaces, fractures, markings and stamps; include a scale reference (ruler, coin, glove); take several angles; keep the camera steady to avoid blur; and use at least about 1024 pixels on the long side. Clean a small patch of surface if the item is dirty or painted. Photos support sorting decisions but never replace physical tests for grade or composition.

## Scrap pricing factors
Scrap prices change daily and differ by region, buyer and volume, so ScrapTrust does not quote prices. Value is driven by the metal type and grade, purity and contamination, preparation (cut to size, sorted, baled), quantity, exchange reference prices (for example LME prices for copper, aluminium, nickel, lead and zinc), transport costs and local demand. Check current prices with local buyers or market reports.

## Reflectance spectroscopy basics (VNIR-SWIR)
Visible–near-infrared (VNIR, about 400–1000 nm) and short-wave infrared (SWIR, about 1000–2500 nm) reflectance spectroscopy identifies minerals from absorption features caused by electronic transitions (iron and other transition metals, mainly below 1300 nm) and molecular vibrations (OH, H2O, metal–OH bonds, carbonate, mainly 1300–2500 nm). Spectra are usually continuum-removed so that feature position, depth and shape can be compared. Feature wavelength identifies the bond type; depth relates to abundance and grain size; shape distinguishes similar minerals.

## Key mineral absorption features
Approximate diagnostic positions: OH near 1400 nm; molecular water near 1900 nm (a 1900 nm feature together with 1400 nm indicates water-bearing minerals); Al-OH near 2160–2220 nm (kaolinite, muscovite/illite, montmorillonite, alunite); Fe-OH near 2250–2290 nm (jarosite, nontronite, some chlorites); Mg-OH near 2300–2350 nm (chlorite, talc, serpentine, amphiboles); carbonate CO3 near 2300–2350 nm (calcite about 2340 nm, dolomite about 2320 nm) with a second feature near 2500 nm; ferric iron (Fe3+) near 850–950 nm (hematite about 860–880 nm, goethite about 900–930 nm); ferrous iron (Fe2+) as broad features near 1000–1100 nm (olivine, pyroxene).

## Distinguishing kaolinite from muscovite and other Al-OH minerals
Kaolinite shows a characteristic doublet in the Al-OH region, at about 2165 and 2205 nm, and also a doublet near 1395 and 1415 nm. Muscovite and illite show a single sharp Al-OH feature near 2200 nm (shifting from about 2190 to 2225 nm with composition) with secondary features near 2350 and 2440 nm. Montmorillonite (smectite) has a broader Al-OH feature near 2205 nm and a strong 1900 nm water feature. Alunite has features near 1760 and 2165 nm. Mixtures and fine grain sizes blur these differences, so interpretation needs good signal-to-noise and, ideally, XRD confirmation.

## Mineral spectral model in this project
The mineral engine interprets VNIR–SWIR reflectance spectra (CSV or XLSX with a wavelength column covering 400–2500 nm). It combines a rule-based absorption-feature engine (163 feature definitions and 58 composite rules, not trained), a PCA plus K-Means model fitted without labels on 170 spectra from 35 rocks, and comparison with similar reference spectra. Results are labelled OBSERVED (features seen, no mineral inferred) or INFERRED (a mineral or mineral group is supported). Its confidence is an evidence-agreement score from 0 to 1, not a probability, because no ground-truth mineral labels exist for the dataset. Confirm important identifications with XRD or petrography.

## What ScrapTrust Copilot is and what it can do
ScrapTrust Copilot is an AI assistant for scrap, waste, recycling and mineral analysis, built for ACE Green Recycling. It answers questions about scrap grades, waste sorting, battery and e-waste safety, recycling processes and mineral spectroscopy. It analyses attached photos (dataset similarity, unsupervised clusters, image quality), videos (key frames plus a transcript of the soundtrack), audio and voice messages (local speech-to-text), PDF and text documents, and reflectance spectra. It shows the evidence from each tool, flags hazards and recommends physical tests. It does not answer questions unrelated to scrap, waste, recycling or minerals.

## The waste image dataset
The waste dataset contains 11,521 images in nine source categories: E-waste, automobile wastes, battery waste, glass waste, light bulbs, metal waste, organic waste, paper waste and plastic waste, split into train and test folders. The categories come from folder names and have not been verified by people, so the Copilot treats them as source labels, not ground truth. A Phase 3 audit found duplicate and near-duplicate groups (some crossing train and test) and kept them in the test split.

## How photo analysis works
Each photo is converted into a 384-dimension embedding with the pretrained DINOv2 ViT-S/14 vision model. The Copilot finds the most similar dataset images (cosine similarity), weights their categories into a vote, reports the agreement level (strong, moderate or weak), finds the nearest of eight K-Means clusters fitted without labels, and flags low-similarity photos as possibly out of distribution. Measured on the dataset with duplicates excluded, the neighbour vote matches the folder category for 92.3% of images, rising to 97.4% when agreement is strong; weak agreement is right only about 44% of the time. The clusters themselves separate categories weakly (silhouette about 0.05).

## Reviewing answers and verified labels
Every answer has thumbs up and thumbs down buttons and, for photo answers, a "Correct the category" option. Reviews are stored with the image hash, the model's predicted category and the reviewer's verified category. They can be exported from /api/v1/copilot/feedback/export and used as ground truth to evaluate the model or train a supervised classifier, which removes the biggest limitation of the current dataset.

## Supported inputs, voice and file types
Click + in the message box to attach photos (JPEG, PNG, WebP), videos (MP4, MOV, WebM and similar; four key frames are analysed and speech is transcribed), audio (MP3, WAV, M4A, OGG, WebM), documents (PDF, TXT, Markdown, CSV) and spectra (CSV or XLSX with a wavelength column). Click the microphone to dictate: the recording is transcribed locally with Whisper and placed in the message box for you to send. Audio never leaves the server for transcription.

## AI providers and offline mode
The Copilot can use Anthropic Claude, OpenAI, Google Gemini or any OpenAI-compatible endpoint (Ollama, LM Studio, Groq, OpenRouter). Add a key such as ANTHROPIC_API_KEY to backend/.env and restart. Without a key it runs in offline mode: the analysis engines and this knowledge base still work, but answers are assembled from tool results and knowledge-base entries instead of being written by an LLM.
